## Shot Count & Pacing Estimates

- **Recommended daily shot count: 10–12 shots**, based on Sam's experience on comparable shoots. 
- Breakdown of a 12-hour shoot day: ~1 hour lunch = 11 working hours; ~2 hours setup at day start = ~9 productive hours; average **~45 minutes per shot**, accounting for blocking, camera moves, and resets. 
- Long takes inherently take more time per shot because every run-through is extended and resets are slower. 
- Inserts (nature, props, hands, etc.) can be captured quickly — often in **5–10 minutes** — and can be slotted between setups without disrupting the day. 
- Current confirmed shot count through Scenes 1–4: **~13 shots**. 
- Projected **total shot count: ~30–36 shots** across all 8 scenes, making a 3-day schedule at 12 shots/day feasible. 
- Sam noted that 36 shots is "completely fine" and encouraged the team to identify **5–10 "nice-to-have" bonus shots** to attempt if time permits, without compromising the core story.

## Scene 1 — Shot-by-Shot Breakdown

**Scene 1 is the most technically demanding sequence (~8–9 shots, ~3.5 pages of script).**

- **Shot 1 (Opening / Dolly Push):** Starts wide and static with the man pacing in front of a bench, voiceover of the letter he is writing begins. An audible "tisk" sound represents him scratching out words. After a few paces, he sits on the bench; at that moment the camera begins a **slow dolly push** toward him (~10–15 feet of travel). Shot ends **over his right shoulder**, tilting slightly down to reveal the notebook. 
    - Key concern: the road/path leading to the bench is **concrete with a possible slight slope**. Options discussed: (a) run dolly directly on concrete (risk of shakiness), (b) lay **dance floors** to smooth the surface, or (c) lay **tracks and remove them in VFX** in post. 
    - Sam noted that on a **wide/medium lens (~30mm)**, legibility of the writing over the shoulder may be limited; team agreed that seeing writing is sufficient — **exact legibility is not a priority**, as voiceover carries the content. 
    - Sam recommended scouting whether the concrete is smooth enough to roll on without tracks before committing to a VFX removal approach. 
- **Shot 2 (Man Looks Up / Pan to Woman):** Man loses his place searching for a French word; head rises as he thinks. Woman's voice says the word — first time he does not react, second time he looks to his left (camera right). Camera then **pedestal or tilt down** to the notebook, then **pans right** across the ground (catching leaves/wind movement), continues panning to reveal the woman's legs, slowly **tilts up** to finish on her sitting on a bollard. 
    - Originally the script placed the woman on a parallel bench; team decided a **bollard** is preferable to avoid a "cheesy" symmetry. 
- **Shots 3 & 4 (Conversation Coverage — Man's and Woman's Angles):** The bulk of Scene 1's dialogue is covered by these two shots, which **intentionally break the 180-degree rule**. 
    - Man's angle: camera positioned on the axis line perpendicular to the bench, capturing a **side profile** of the man as he looks toward the woman's direction.
    - Woman's angle: camera **behind and slightly to the side** of the woman, showing only one side of her face as she speaks.
    - Deliberate choice: the audience should **never have spatial certainty** about where the woman is relative to the man — whether she is real, imagined, or a ghostly figure. 
    - Team acknowledged the unconventional cutting may feel jarring and plans to **test it in practice** before committing. 
- **Shot 5 (Establishing / Spatial Context):** At some point in the scene, a shot that reveals both characters in the same frame, establishing their relative positions — used sparingly so as not to fully resolve the spatial ambiguity. 
- **Shot 6 (Tilt Down + Follow Pan):** From the man's face, camera tilts down to the notebook; man stands up and the camera **pans to follow him** as he walks a few steps closer to the view. Camera position does not change — only tilt and pan movement. 
- **Shot 7 (Pull Focus):** Starts with man dominating frame left, woman soft/out-of-focus in background. A strong gust of wind causes both to react; man looks right, audible paper crinkle. Camera then **pulls focus** to the woman in the background. 
- **Shot 8 (Woman Exits / Pan to Nature / Woman Reappears):** Woman walks off frame (direction TBD — likely camera right). Camera **pans right** across nature (trees, leaves, open landscape) without the woman in frame. As the pan is still moving, the woman **reappears** — entering from either the same or opposite side, creating a naturalistic but slightly disorienting sense of her movement. 
    - Key creative intent: her reappearance should feel **naturalistic, not ghostly**, but the audience should momentarily lose track of her. 
    - She then looks back toward the man's direction, delivering her final line of the scene.
- **Scene 1 Closing — Paper Flies Away:** A shot of the bench with the paper on it; paper flies off. Team has recruited friends to engineer a **wind/fan rig** to make the paper appear to be genuinely caught in the wind (not just fall to the ground). 
    - May also capture a shot of the paper **in the air** for editorial flexibility. 
    - Possible cut back to woman saying: *"It has a rebellious turn of mind,"* followed by the sound of the man's footsteps chasing the paper.

## Cinematography & Visual Approach

- **Priority is cinematic language over dialogue fidelity**: shooting a simplified version of the script would fundamentally change the film's visual identity, so pulling off the intended shots takes precedence. 
- **Full ADR (Automated Dialogue Replacement)** planned: actors will not be cut for dialogue errors during shooting. Cuts will be driven by camera/composition issues only. This reduces pressure and allows more takes focused on movement and blocking. 
- **Blocking rehearsal is critical**: actors (especially Holly and Tyler) must know their exact positions and movements for each shot, since long takes require precise choreography. 
- Sam emphasized the importance of **shot variation** — not staying exclusively wide. Closer shots (mediums, close-ups) help audiences connect emotionally, especially in scenes 2–4 which currently skew wide. 
- **Forrest Gump** referenced as a tonal/compositional touchpoint for the bench-centered storytelling, though the team's approach involves more camera movement and intentional spatial ambiguity. 
- **Hamnet** cited as a visual reference for slow, nature-oriented cinematography and meditative framing. 
- **Train to Dreams** (Netflix) also referenced for strong nature photography. 
- Sam noted that **production design can do as much work as gear** — citing his recent shoot with director Darren, where a rented 1960s bus, natural lighting, and two tube lights produced visually compelling results with minimal equipment. 
- Team agreed: **lighting will be primarily natural** given the outdoor setting; camera movement and production design are the primary tools for visual impact. 
- **ShotDeck** has been used to pull reference stills; team to share reference films and clipped shots with Sam before Thursday.
