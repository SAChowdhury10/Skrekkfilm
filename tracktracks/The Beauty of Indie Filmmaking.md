# UFI Kickstarter Pre-Shoot: Speaker Monologues


---

## 1. On the Beauty of Indie Filmmaking

### Shafi

There is something about doing it yourself. There is an abundance of thought and very little machinery. Not much stands between what you dreamed and what ends up on the screen. Nobody is rewriting your grammar. Nobody is telling you the ending is too quiet. You get to keep the strange parts. You keep the parts that would make a producer cough. Every choice is yours to defend. Every mistake is yours to sit with. It is a haphazard affair, and that is what makes it feel true. It starts with a guttural response and follows wherever that pulls you, even if the pulling takes years.

### Ishmam

When we were kids and we played, we already knew something about ourselves. Something in that play knew. You did not need permission then. You did not need a budget. You just moved. Maybe this kind of filmmaking is trying to get back to that. It's doing loud work in quiet rooms with people who show up because they believe in something they cannot fully name. You are given the clearance to say whatever you want. Most people never receive that kind of a gift. Most people spend their entire lives translating themselves into a language somebody else made.

### Salman

Most of filmmaking is invisible. You are just showing up on a Tuesday to help move a light stand. But something is being built. On bigger productions, those instincts can sometimes get lost. There is often a voice from above saying: let's do it faster, let's do it cheaper, let's make it more digestible. Here nobody is doing that. Here the strangeness gets to breathe.

### Tyler

You always have that feeling that the world is in front of you. It's there in every take. It's there in every setup. There is this openness to the day that gets flattened when there is too much money on the table. Suddenly everyone is scared. Here nobody is scared, or at least, nobody is scared of the same things. We are scared of getting it wrong on our own terms. That is the fear I signed up for. I would rather chase that than sit in a trailer waiting for someone to tell me what my character wants for lunch.

### Holly

There is a version of every film that only exists before all the notes come in. That version is usually the truest one. Indie work protects that version. 

### Devin

In indie filmmaking, talent never goes unnoticed. to be part of something big and came back saying they were mostly waiting, waiting to be used, waiting for permission. Indie work asks the opposite of you. It asks you to bring everything, all the time. It is a haphazard affair, and it will exhaust you in ways you didn't know you could be exhausted. But at the end you have made a thing, a thing that has your fingerprints on it. That is worth the tiredness, every time.

### Ismam

Nobody hands you a call sheet with your name already important on it. You write yourself into it, or you don't show up at all. That's the whole difference between here and everywhere else I've worked. On a big set you wait for someone to notice you're useful. Here you're useful before you've proven anything, because there's nobody else around to do the job. I like that math. It's terrifying and it's honest at the same time.

### Samiha

My hands smell like gaffer tape and coffee by the second week, and I've started to like it. There is a particular kind of tired that comes from solving problems all day with things that aren't quite the right tool, and I've decided that's my favorite kind of tired. Nothing on this set was designed for us. We designed it back, out of whatever showed up in the van that morning. That is the actual craft, more than anything else. You learn what a scene needs by not having what you thought you needed. The work gets more honest once your own hands are the ones fixing it.

### Sana

I keep thinking about how much this reminds me of being a kid, building a whole world out of whatever was lying around the yard. Indie filmmaking is the adult version of that, except now the yard is other people's time, so you have to be a little more careful with it. Still, the impulse is the same one. Nobody told you to build something out of nothing. You just did it, because not building felt worse than the risk of it going badly. I don't think that impulse ever really leaves a person. It just waits around for someone to hand you a camera again.

Everyone here is going to tell you something poetic about freedom, and I don't disagree with any of it, I'll just also mention that indie filmmaking has meant a granola bar for lunch four days running. There's a real beauty in that, weirdly. Strip away enough comfort and what's left is just the reason you're actually here. Nobody sticks around for craft services on a shoot like this one. They stick around because they want to see the thing get made. That's a purer motive than most jobs run on, and I say that as someone who has absolutely complained about the granola bars out loud.

### Nick

Every film gets made twice, once in the writing and once in whatever the budget lets you keep. Indie filmmaking is the version where you get to decide what stays. Most people never get to make that decision even once in their working lives, and we make it here most mornings before the coffee kicks in. I don't romanticize the lack of money. I just notice what it protects. It protects the strange choice nobody in a bigger room would have signed off on. Cinema takes your time regardless of the budget behind it. It might as well take it on our own terms.

## 2. What is the Meaning of Great Art

### Shafi

You must sacrifice reality to be a great artist. I did not want to believe that for a long time. Every time I look at work that has moved me, I can see what the person gave up to make it: time, sanity, sometimes the people they loved. It is a constant cycle between self-care and self-destruction. Great art is what survives that cycle without pretending the cycle wasn't real. It holds irony and sincerity in tension. That tension is what makes the good stuff feel alive on the wall or on the screen. Take that tension away and you get decoration. Decoration is fine. It is just not the thing I am after.

### Ishmam

Great art is giving your most dangerous thoughts a welcome home: the thoughts you would not say at dinner, the thoughts you would not say to your mother. You build them a room. You furnish the room carefully so a stranger can walk in and sit down and feel understood. That is the whole trick. The closest we get to Heaven, Heaven must be reconciliation of some sort. And art is where the reconciliation happens in advance. You reconcile with the thing in yourself first. Then somebody else finds the room you built and reconciles with the same thing in themselves, all of it in silence.

Of all the great works of art I've experienced, the consistent factor they all share is that there is an undeniable allure to them. They pull you in, attract you, and make you ruminate over them. There is a haunting quality to them. They are both everything you ever wanted and still somehow not enough, so you keep returning to them over and over. Impossible to exhaust in a single encounter.

It feels like the artist wasn't just making something—they were trying to bring something out that already existed within them. There is a feeling of necessity, like the work had to come into the world.

### Tyler

"Have I embarrassed you?" That is a great gauge of good art. If a piece has never embarrassed you, it probably has not done its job. Great work makes you feel exposed even when it is not about you. Subtlety isn't derived from the meaning, subtlety is derived from the making. You can have a huge, loud subject and still make it with such care that the whole thing whispers. That is the kind of work I want to be part of, the kind that whispers something you already knew but never had the words for. I don't have time for the fear of death. I have other matters to attend to. That is the posture, I think. That is what shows up in the frame.

### Holly
When I think about the best works of art, there is a sense that the artist had to make it that way. They weren't thinking about whether people would like it, whether it would be popular, or whether it would fit into what was expected of them. They were just following the work and being faithful to what it needed to become. I think that's what makes great art feel so honest. You can feel that nothing was added just to please people and nothing was taken away out of fear.

### Nick

All art is palliative. The only cure is maybe death. I do not say that to be grim. I say it because it takes the pressure off. Art is not going to fix anything. What it will do is make the ache more bearable while you carry it. That is enough. That is a lot. Time is the engine of life and life is the engine of cinema. Cinema takes your time and gives you a shape for it, a shape you can hold, a shape you can share. Ornaments of the soul, some old writer called them. That keeps me humble about what we are actually doing.

When I think about the best works of art, I think one of the things that stands out is how rare they are. A lot of things can be impressive, entertaining, or meaningful, but very few things reach that level where everything comes together.

Art comes to you when that feeling of being trapped sets in, when home isn't enough but nobody outside wants anything to do with you. That is the doorway. It is not a pleasant doorway. It is the one. Participation requires an understanding of responsibility. If you are going to walk through the doorway you carry something back. You do not just stand in the ache and describe it. You bring the ache into a form that other people can hold. Free their mind and the people will free themselves. That is the whole project. Let it stew, then give it a stir when you're ready. Nobody hands you a schedule for that. You show up when the stew is ready.

When I think about the best works of art, there is a feeling that everything belongs together. Nothing feels random or disconnected. Every choice feels like it came from the same place, like the entire work is being guided by one clear vision. Even if there are many different elements, they all feel like they are part of the same whole. That's what makes the work feel complete—it doesn't feel like a collection of ideas, it feels like one living thing.

### Ismam

I used to think great art had to be difficult to be good. I don't believe that anymore. Some of the best stuff I've seen is embarrassingly simple, and it still catches you somewhere you weren't ready for. Great art doesn't ask permission to affect you. It just does, and then you spend the car ride home trying to figure out why. I think that's the actual test, if there is one. Not whether you understood it. Whether it followed you out of the room.

When I think about the best works of art, I feel like they come from someone who truly understands the thing they are working with. They are not just trying to express an idea—they have spent years learning the language of their craft.

### Samiha

I think great art is made the way you make anything with your hands, badly at first and then less badly and then, on a good day, not badly at all. Nobody tells you in advance which day that will be. You just keep showing up with the same tools and see what they let you do this time. The mistake is thinking the idea is the art. The idea is just the reason you picked up the tool in the first place. Everything true happens in the doing, in the small adjustments nobody will ever notice but you. Care is the only ingredient in this whole process I actually trust.

When I think about the best works of art, I don't think they come from inspiration alone. There is always a level of discipline behind them. The artist has put in the time to understand their craft so deeply that they can turn something internal into something real.

### Sana

Great art is the thing you still remember years later, long after you've forgotten what year it was. It detaches from its own timeline and just becomes part of how you think. I've forgotten entire relationships and kept a single line from a film I saw once, at the wrong age, alone. That says something about what actually lasts. It isn't the polish, it's whatever snuck past your defenses while you weren't being careful. I want to make the kind of thing that sneaks past someone the way it once snuck past me.

I don't have a grand theory of great art. I know it when something I watched at midnight is still bothering me at breakfast. That's about the extent of my critical framework, and it has never once failed me. Most things don't survive breakfast. The ones that do are usually the ones people were too scared to make in the first place. I'd rather chase that feeling than any theory about it. Theories are for people who've stopped letting themselves be surprised.

## 3. On UFI (the film)

### Shafi

It is the fleeting resurrection of the past. That is what I keep circling around when I try to say what this film is. People ask me how I came up with the idea. I was sitting on a bench. It got hot as the sun came out. I found shade so I could feel chilly again. I was chasing the chill. That is the whole seed. When my desire for validation subsided, I found something perhaps just as strong lurking underneath: my desire to take something all the way as far as it can go. That is where my drive comes from. Why specifically writing, that remains a mystery. I have to make the words bring back a youth. That is how I show what has been lost.

### Ishmam

Even though this is co-directed, we both feel as if it is a monological self expression. Two people wrote it and two people are shooting it and somehow the film only has one voice. This film is the sound of pondering. It is a mix of philosophy and tomfoolery. It is not a science of enjoyment, rather an experience of truth. It will require you to go to your own memory. Beckett once said, I hope the peculiar thing never fails you, whatever it is, that gives you a moment's solace in a chaotic world. That is what I want the film to be for someone: a small peculiar thing. It would take a giant leap of verbal dexterity to explain myself further. We shopped the idea around the subdivision and got a lot of confused looks. Morning shouldn't last any longer than the night.

### Ismam

I read the script before I knew anyone attached to it, which might be the purest way to read anything. It didn't explain itself, and I liked that it didn't. Most scripts are so afraid of losing you that they explain the joke twice. This one trusts you to sit in the confusion for a minute. I signed on because of one page, honestly, page eleven, where nothing happens and everything happens. I still don't have better words for it than that.

### Samiha

I came onto this not knowing the whole shape of it, just a few pages and a feeling I couldn't shake, which is usually how I know something is worth building. My job is to make the world in the script feel like it was always there, like we just found it and started filming. Half of that is research and half of it is instinct, and I genuinely couldn't tell you the ratio. What I know is that when it's working, the set stops feeling like a set. It starts feeling like a place someone actually lived in. That's the whole job, honestly.

### Tyler

This film feels like it's trying to hold onto something before it's gone, and I think that's true of most of the small films I actually love. You can feel the clock in it, not as urgency but as tenderness, like someone gently trying to remember a face before they leave the room. I don't want to explain the plot, because the plot isn't really the point. The point is closer to what it feels like to miss something you haven't lost yet. If that makes sense to you already, you'll understand the film immediately.

### Holly

When they first described this film to me I understood about sixty percent of it and said yes anyway, which is more or less my whole strategy for most of my good decisions. I've since understood a lot more of it, and I like it more, not less, which doesn't always happen. It's a film that trusts you to sit with something instead of explaining it to you twice. I respect that. I also occasionally miss things being explained to me twice. Both of those are true at once.

### Nick

I don't know how to summarize what this film is about, and I've stopped trying at parties. What I can tell you is that it moves at the speed of an actual thought, not the speed a thought gets edited down to for other people's patience. Time is the engine of life and life is the engine of cinema, I said that somewhere else and I still believe it here. This film spends its time on things that don't usually get any. A held breath, say. Nobody asked me to defend that decision, so I won't. Watch it and see where your own thoughts drift. That drift is the film working.

## 4. On Tyler and Holly (our actors)

### Ishmam

The frustration of the desire to make this film was a small technical difficulty. We thought of them quite immediately, in the first hours. They made it easy. Tyler's' pomp can be seen from across the room. For Holly, you have to see her in her personality. Both of them are able to take a scene from the universal, into the particular, and back into the universal. That is a rare movement. Most actors can only do one direction. Very few can do the return trip. Artists risk public excoriation, so it is very brave of them to say yes to a film like this, a film that does not explain itself, a film that asks them to be still and let something happen underneath the stillness.

### Shafi

The most harmful thing is demanding from people to be what they are not. So we wanted to explain little to them of the meaning. Instead of trying to make someone smile we hope we can make them do something that they uniquely do, which is equivalent to smiling. Tyler brings something I could not have written. Holly brings something I could not have written. Together they arrived at a room we built without any of us discussing the floor plan. It was such an inspired moment when I first saw them read together. Art resists classification, as do artists. Both of them refuse to be one thing. That refusal is exactly what the film needs.

### Salman

I've worked with actors who perform at you. Tyler and Holly aren't doing that. They're doing something closer to listening, even in scenes where they're supposed to be the one talking. You forget the camera is there, which almost never happens to me anymore. Holly can hold a silence longer than anyone I've worked with and make it feel like the loudest thing in the room. Tyler will surprise you in the middle of a take you thought you already understood. I don't know how to explain it better than that. Watch them and you'll see it yourself.

### Samiha

I dress a set around what an actor is going to do in it before I've ever actually seen them do it, which is a strange kind of trust to extend to a stranger. With Tyler and Holly it never felt like a gamble. You could tell from the read-through which corners of the room they'd end up using. That's a gift, knowing a space before you've been given a reason to. I built rooms for both of them without knowing exactly what they'd do inside those rooms, and every time, what they did was better than what I'd imagined. That almost never happens.

### Sana

I've watched a lot of actors try to perform a memory, and it almost never works, because memory doesn't perform, it just surfaces. Tyler and Holly let things surface. You can see it happen on their faces in real time, something arriving instead of something delivered. I don't know how to teach that, and I don't think you can. You can only recognize it when it's in front of you and be grateful somebody brought it into the room.

I've been on set with actors who need seventeen takes to find something real, and actors who find it on the first one and then spend sixteen takes trying to get back to it. Tyler and Holly are neither. They just seem to bring it with them, already loaded, like they packed it that morning along with their coffee. It's a little annoying if I'm honest, mostly because it makes my job of staying focused a lot easier, and I don't get to complain about anything.

### Nick

Directing is mostly deciding who to trust and then getting out of their way. Tyler and Holly made that decision easy. I've watched actors chase a feeling and never catch it. These two just let the feeling catch them, on camera, in real time. That is rarer than people think, and it's the only thing I actually care about when I watch dailies. Everything else is craft you can learn. That isn't.

## 5. On Why We Need Your Help

### Shafi

We are asking for three thousand dollars. It is a modest number for a film and a large number for the people we are asking. I know that. Every dollar that shows up in the campaign becomes a light we could not have afforded, a meal for a crew member, a permit that keeps us from being chased out of a park. This is a small ask that lets a small film exist. If you have five dollars, we have use for it. If you have five hundred, we have use for that too. Mostly we are asking you to say yes to a thing that has no reason to exist except that we could not stop thinking about it.

I'm usually the person in the room asking whether we actually need something, so it should mean something that I'm here asking you for money instead of cutting the budget further. We already cut the things that were fine to cut. What's left is the stuff that actually holds the film together, so this isn't a padded ask. If it were, I'd tell you, because that's more or less my whole personality. Give if you can. I promise it's going where we said it would.

### Ishmam

We could make this film with less. We are trying not to. The reason has nothing to do with luxury. It has to do with wanting to pay the people who show up. That is where the money goes. Into hands that carry the film across the finish line. If you give, you are supporting the person holding the boom pole in a cold October field, the person driving the dolly, the person feeding all of us at two in the morning. Kickstarter is a strange ritual. You ask strangers to believe in something before it exists. We are asking you to believe in this. Whatever amount feels right. We will remember it.

### Salman

A small film is a fragile thing. It gets made because a lot of small yeses stack up in a row. Your yes is one of those. It does not need to be a large yes. What we are trying to protect with this money is time: time on location without rushing, time to get the shot right instead of the shot that fits, time to feed the crew properly so the tenth hour of the day still feels like the first. That is what the number on the page really means: time. If you give us a little, you are giving us a little more of that. And we will not waste it.

### Tyler

I am an actor in this film and I am also a person on the internet asking you for money. I want to be honest about how strange that is. But I would not be in front of this camera asking if I did not believe in what we are making. This is the kind of project that will exist because a small number of people cared enough to fund it. If it does not get funded, it does not happen. It is that direct. So if this speaks to you, give what you can. If it does not, that is fine too. Send it to a friend who might feel differently. That helps almost as much.

### Holly

There is a reason the ask is not larger. We do not want to make a bigger version of this film. We want to make this version, at this size, with these people. What the pledge does is protect the smallness. It lets us stay careful. It lets us not cut corners on the parts that would show. If you have room to give, I would love your name on the list of people who made this possible. If you do not, I understand entirely. Money is heavy right now for a lot of people. The best thing you can do in that case is watch the film when it comes out and tell someone about it.

I think of a Kickstarter pledge as a form of witness. You are saying, in advance, that you want a thing to exist. That is not a small gesture. Films need witnesses long before they have audiences. We are looking for those early witnesses. The ones who will vouch for something they have not yet seen. If that sounds like you, we would be grateful for whatever you can spare. Even the smallest tier moves us closer. And if you cannot give money, share the link. Tell one person about the film. That is also witness. That is also enough.

### Devin

Every crew member on this project is a volunteer. Every one of us has a day job. What we are asking money for is the parts of a film that a volunteer cannot cover with time: insurance, permits, rentals, food, the physical infrastructure that lets the volunteering happen at all. If you give, you are funding the ground underneath the paycheck we are all forgoing. That framing helps me every time I share the link. Maybe it helps you too. Whatever you can offer, we will put it directly toward keeping this thing standing until October.

### Nick

There are a lot of things asking for your money right now. I know. I get the same emails you do. The reason to give to this one, if you are going to give to anything, is that a small independent film is one of the few places where a dollar still buys something irreplaceable. It buys a frame that would not otherwise exist. It buys a scene that would have been cut. That is a strange kind of return. It is a real one. If you have five dollars to give to an art project this month, I hope you consider giving them here. It is coming into being and passing back into obsolescence, all of it fast. We are trying to catch a little of it before it goes.

This is the part where I am supposed to make you feel something. I do not want to. The film itself will do that, if we get to finish it. What I want to say is much simpler. We need help. We would like your help. If you can give, thank you. If you cannot, thank you for reading this far. That already means something. Kickstarter is all or nothing. If we do not hit the goal, nothing comes through. So if you are on the fence, the tip of the scale really is on you. That is the whole pitch. There is no clever closer. Just a hand held out.

### Ismam

I've never asked strangers for money before this. It feels strange every single time I hit send. But I keep doing it because I've seen what three thousand dollars actually buys on a set like ours, and it's not abstract. It's gas money and gaffer tape and a proper meal instead of gas station sandwiches on day two. If you can give ten dollars, give ten dollars. If you can't, that's fine too, just watch the film when it's out and tell somebody it exists. Either one helps more than you'd think.

### Samiha

People don't think about the department that buys nails and fabric and paint until it runs out, and then they think about it constantly, usually mid-shoot, usually at the worst time. That is what a chunk of this budget protects. Not glamour, just enough material to build the world properly the first time so we aren't gluing it back together on day four. If you have ever noticed a small detail in a film, something in the background you weren't even supposed to catch, that detail had a budget line. I'm asking you to fund the details nobody will consciously see and everybody will feel.

### Sana

My grandmother used to save small amounts of money in envelopes labeled for things that hadn't happened yet. I think about that a lot during this campaign. You're being asked to put a little into an envelope labeled for something that doesn't exist yet, except as a feeling a few of us have been carrying around for a while. That takes a kind of trust I don't take lightly. If you give something, know it's going into the envelope marked exactly what you'd hope: the film, the whole film, nothing else.
